The problem

Attackers do not break in. They log in.

MFA is failing on friction

SMS is SIM-swappable, push is fatigued into approval, codes are relayed by reverse-proxy phishing kits in real time. Every one of them adds a step the user resents.

Passkeys solve the wrong half

They defeat phishing, and they are excellent. They do not tell you whether the person at the keyboard is the person the key was issued to, and they require a device to be registered, carried and replaced.

Agents change the arithmetic

Autonomous software now navigates interfaces, fills forms and stuffs credentials at machine speed. A static secret can no longer demonstrate that a human is present.

Credentials leak continuously

Infostealers harvest them by the billion. The valuable question stopped being what the secret is, and became who or what is entering it.

The solution

A second factor the user never performs.

The client measures dwell and flight timings from the passphrase the user was going to type anyway, and folds them into the key derivation. A login succeeds only when the credential is right and the cadence matches the enrolled baseline. Nothing is added to the flow, because the factor is the flow.

The engine, the maths and the threat model in full →

  • Zero added steps. No hardware, no authenticator app, no second prompt.
  • Leaked credentials neutralised. The dump does not contain a cadence.
  • Bot-resistant by construction. Synthetic input has no neuromuscular variance.
  • Zero-knowledge. The server holds ciphertext and an Argon2id hash; it cannot read a vault.
  • Never a lockout. A bad day is an amber retype, then a Backup Code. The body is not a gate.

Free for a single device under ten sites. Paid tiers add sync, encrypted offline cache, breach checking and anomaly alerts. Pricing is published and shipped at M4.

The factor as a service

An embeddable SDK letting any product add cadence verification to its own login, with Progressive Enrollment building a profile from the logins users already perform. M5.

Why this founder, why now

Built once in 2007, shelved for the right reason, and now unshelved for a better one.

The first version was a telemetry script inside a custom MVC framework, written to tell humans from scrapers. It turned out to tell people apart instead — demonstrated by writing a password on a whiteboard and watching a room full of engineers fail to log in with it.

It was shelved because smartphones flattened the signal it depended on. It is back because the threat moved: physical keyboards remain where credentials are entered at work, and automated credential abuse is now the dominant intrusion path.
